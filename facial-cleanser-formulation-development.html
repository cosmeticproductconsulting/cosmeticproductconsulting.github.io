What Is Facial Cleanser Formulation?

Quick answer: Facial cleanser formulation is the process of designing a skincare product that removes dirt, oil, makeup and impurities from the facial skin without causing unnecessary dryness or irritation. It involves decisions about surfactant selection, cleansing performance, foam profile, pH, viscosity, preservation, sensory experience and packaging compatibility.

Facial cleansing is the first step in almost every skincare routine. It sets the stage for everything that follows. If the cleanser strips the skin, the moisturiser has to work harder. If it leaves residue, the serum cannot absorb properly. If it irritates, the entire routine becomes uncomfortable. That is why formulation matters more than most people realise.

The word "cleanser" covers a wide range of product formats. A gel cleanser, a cream cleanser, a foaming face wash, a cleansing oil, a micellar water, a cleansing balm and a cleansing milk are all cleansers. But they are formulated according to very different logic. A gel cleanser relies on a surfactant system that foams and rinses cleanly. A cream cleanser uses a milder surfactant system combined with emollients. A cleansing oil works on the principle of like dissolves like. A micellar water uses very mild surfactants in a water base.

Understanding these differences is the starting point of facial cleanser development. If you want to see how cleanser formulation fits into the broader picture of cosmetic product development, read our detailed guide on cosmetic product development.

Why Facial Cleanser Formulation Is Different From Serum or Cream Formulation

A serum, a cream and a cleanser are all cosmetic products. But they are formulated according to very different principles.

A serum is a leave-on product. Its job is to deliver active ingredients to the skin over time. Formulation focuses on solubility, pH, absorption, sensory feel and long-term stability on the skin. A serum that pills, feels sticky or oxidises is a failed serum, no matter how good the ingredient list looks.

A cream is also a leave-on product. Its job is to moisturise, protect and support the skin barrier. Formulation focuses on the oil phase, emulsifier system, thickeners and emollients. Texture, stability and sensory feel are critical because the product stays on the skin for hours.

A cleanser is a rinse-off product. Its job is to remove dirt, oil and makeup, then disappear down the drain. Formulation focuses on surfactant systems that clean effectively without stripping the skin. The product does not stay on the skin, so the bar for long-term skin compatibility is different from a leave-on product. But the short-term experience — how the skin feels immediately after rinsing — matters enormously.

Who Needs Professional Facial Cleanser Formulation?

Professional cleanser formulation is relevant for anyone who wants a cleanser that works — not just a cleanser that exists. That includes:

  • New skincare brands developing a hero cleanser from scratch.
  • Established brands reformulating an existing face wash or launching a new one.
  • Dermatologists and aesthetic clinics creating signature aftercare or retail cleansers.
  • Private label businesses seeking customisation beyond a generic base.
  • Cosmetic manufacturers needing formulation support for client projects.
  • Entrepreneurs developing a differentiated hero product.

The development approach adapts to the stage of the project, from an early concept to reformulation or preparation for manufacturing.

Custom Development

Custom Facial Cleanser Formulation

Every skincare brand has a different cleansing philosophy. A startup may want a simple, gentle gel. An established brand may need a sophisticated cream cleanser with multiple skin-conditioning ingredients.

Quick answer: Custom facial cleanser formulation develops the product around your specific concept, target consumer, cleansing preference, sensory goals, price segment, packaging format and market requirements. It does not force your idea into a generic base.

Custom formulation starts with your brief. The formulator then builds the cleanser around what you actually want the product to do. Depending on the intended product, formulation development may consider:

  • Skin type and consumer profile — oily, dry, combination, sensitive, acne-prone, mature.
  • Desired cleansing performance — gentle daily cleanse, deep clean, makeup removal, exfoliating.
  • Target texture and sensory experience — gel, cream, foam, oil, milk, balm, micellar water.
  • Ingredient preferences — natural, botanical, sulfate-free, fragrance-free, vegan.
  • Intended price segment — mass, masstige, prestige, luxury.
  • Packaging format — pump, tube, flip-top bottle, airless, jar, sachet.
  • Manufacturing requirements — batch size, equipment, MOQ, scalability.
  • Target market and regulatory considerations — India, EU, US, GCC, Southeast Asia.

The result is a formulation strategy designed around the specific product rather than a one-size-fits-all cleanser. This is the difference between a product that stands out and a product that blends into a crowded shelf.

Custom Formulation vs Ready-Made Cleanser Base

A ready-made cleanser base from a supplier is a fast way to launch. But it comes with limited control and limited differentiation. You are essentially using a formula that other brands may also be using, with minor modifications.

Custom formulation works the other way round. You begin with your customer, your positioning and your goals. The formulation is built or adapted around them. You also come away understanding why each choice was made, which helps every time you launch, reformulate or talk to a manufacturer.

Neither route is wrong. Some brands start with a ready-made base to test the market and move to custom once they understand their customers. Others invest in a custom hero cleanser from day one because uniqueness is the whole point of the brand. The important thing is to choose knowingly.

Quick answer: Facial cleanser formulation development follows six stages: concept and positioning, surfactant system selection, cleanser base selection, pH and sensory development, preservation and product protection, and stability and compatibility evaluation. Each stage depends on the one before it.

1. Product Concept and Positioning

The first step is defining what the cleanser is expected to achieve. A product may be positioned around gentle daily cleansing, deep pore cleansing, makeup removal, exfoliation, hydration or blemish control.

The concept influences surfactant selection, product format, sensory profile and development strategy. A gentle gel cleanser for sensitive skin will look very different from a deep-cleansing foaming wash for oily, acne-prone skin. The concept should be specific enough to guide formulation decisions.

Here is a practical tip: write the concept as if you are explaining the product to someone who has never used anything like it. If you cannot explain it clearly, the formulator will not be able to build it clearly.

2. Surfactant System Selection

Surfactants are the heart of any cleanser. They are the ingredients that actually remove oil and dirt from the skin. But not all surfactants are the same. Some are harsh. Some are gentle. Some foam richly. Some barely foam at all. Some are easy to preserve. Some are difficult.

Surfactant selection depends on the desired cleansing strength, foam profile, mildness and skin compatibility. A cleanser for oily skin may use a stronger anionic surfactant system. A cleanser for sensitive skin may use a blend of mild nonionic and amphoteric surfactants. A sulfate-free cleanser avoids sulfated surfactants entirely and relies on alternatives such as glucosides or sarcosinates.

The surfactant system also affects the product's texture, viscosity and rinse feel. Getting this right is the most important technical decision in cleanser formulation.

3. Selecting the Cleanser Base

Different cleansing concepts require different formulation structures. A cleanser may be developed as a:

  • Gel cleanser — clear or translucent, water-based, usually foaming. Suits oily and combination skin. Relies on a surfactant blend plus a gelling agent.
  • Cream cleanser — opaque, lotion-like, often non-foaming or low-foaming. Suits dry and sensitive skin. Combines mild surfactants with emollients.
  • Foaming cleanser — dispensed as a foam from a pump or aerosol. Light, airy, easy to spread. Suits normal and combination skin.
  • Cleansing oil — anhydrous, oil-based. Excellent for makeup removal. Works on the principle of like dissolves like.
  • Cleansing milk — lightweight emulsion. Gentle, creamy, often wipe-off or rinse-off. Suits dry and mature skin.
  • Cleansing balm — solid or semi-solid oil-based product that melts on contact with skin. Excellent for makeup removal and massage.
  • Micellar water — very mild surfactant solution in water. No rinsing required. Suits sensitive skin and quick cleansing.

The choice depends on the skin type, cleansing needs, sensory preferences and technical requirements.

4. pH, Texture and Sensory Development

pH is a critical parameter in cleanser formulation. It affects skin compatibility, preservative efficacy, surfactant performance and product stability. Gentle cleansers typically aim for a pH close to the skin's natural pH (around 5.0–5.5). Soap-based cleansers tend to have higher pH (9.0–10.0) and can be more stripping.

Texture and sensory feel are equally important from a consumer perspective. A cleanser that feels slimy, leaves residue or does not rinse cleanly will not be repurchased, no matter how good the ingredient list looks. Formulation development therefore considers viscosity, foam quality, spreadability, rinse feel, slip and after-feel.

5. Preservation and Product Protection

Products containing water generally require an appropriate preservation strategy to help control microbial contamination. Cleansers can be particularly challenging to preserve because the surfactant system can interact with preservatives, reducing their effectiveness.

Some surfactant-based cleansers are difficult to preserve and may require careful selection of preservative systems. Preservative efficacy testing (challenge testing) confirms that the system is working. The preservation strategy must consider the formula's pH, surfactant type, water activity and packaging.

6. Stability and Compatibility Evaluation

A cleanser needs to remain suitable throughout its intended shelf life and storage conditions. Development may therefore involve evaluating characteristics such as appearance, colour, odour, pH, viscosity, foam quality, physical stability and packaging compatibility.

Cleansers are often stored in bathrooms, where temperature and humidity fluctuate. Stability testing should account for real-world storage conditions, not just controlled laboratory conditions. Products containing natural ingredients or sensitive actives may require additional stability considerations.

Technical Deep Dive

Understanding Surfactants in Facial Cleanser Formulation

Surfactants are the most important ingredient class in any cleanser. Understanding how they work is essential to formulating a product that cleans effectively without damaging the skin.

Quick answer: Surfactants are molecules with a water-loving head and an oil-loving tail. They work by reducing the surface tension between water and oil, allowing oil and dirt to be lifted from the skin and rinsed away. The type, concentration and blend of surfactants determine how a cleanser performs, how it feels and how mild it is on the skin.

How Surfactants Work

Skin produces sebum, an oily substance that helps protect the skin barrier. Dirt, pollution, makeup and dead skin cells also accumulate on the skin surface. Water alone cannot remove these because oil and water do not mix.

Surfactants solve this problem. Their molecular structure has two ends: a hydrophilic (water-loving) head and a lipophilic (oil-loving) tail. The tail attaches to oil and dirt. The head attaches to water. When the cleanser is rinsed away, the oil and dirt are carried with it.

This is why surfactants are called "surface-active agents." They change the surface properties of water, allowing it to wet and penetrate materials it would normally repel.

Choosing the Right Surfactant System

The surfactant system is not just one ingredient. It is a carefully designed blend. Most cleansers use a combination of surfactants to achieve the desired balance of cleansing strength, foam quality, mildness and cost.

For example, a gentle cleanser might combine:

  • Sodium lauroyl sarcosinate — a mild anionic surfactant that provides good cleansing with low irritation.
  • Cocamidopropyl betaine — an amphoteric surfactant that boosts foam and reduces irritation from the anionic surfactant.
  • Decyl glucoside — a nonionic surfactant that adds mildness and a creamy foam.

A stronger cleanser for oily skin might combine sodium laureth sulfate with cocamidopropyl betaine and a small amount of a nonionic surfactant for foam stability.

Mildness vs Cleansing Strength

There is always a trade-off between mildness and cleansing strength. Stronger surfactants clean more effectively but can strip the skin. Milder surfactants are gentler but may not remove heavy makeup or excess oil as efficiently.

The formulator's job is to find the right balance for the intended consumer. A cleanser for sensitive skin should prioritise mildness. A cleanser for oily, acne-prone skin may need stronger cleansing. A daily-use cleanser should be gentle enough for everyday use. A makeup remover needs to dissolve cosmetics without irritating the eyes.

If you are developing a cleanser for the Indian market, where consumers often use multiple skincare products and live in varying climates, the balance between mildness and cleansing strength becomes especially important.

Surfactant Interactions and Formulation Challenges

Surfactants do not work in isolation. They interact with other ingredients in the formula, and these interactions can create challenges.

Some surfactants interact with preservatives, reducing their effectiveness. This is why cleansers can be difficult to preserve. Some surfactants interact with thickeners, affecting viscosity. Some surfactants are sensitive to pH changes. Some surfactants can cause cloudiness or separation in the final product.

Understanding these interactions is a key part of professional cleanser formulation. It is also why trial-and-error formulation rarely produces a reliable result. The formulator needs to understand the chemistry of the system, not just mix ingredients together.

Sulfate-Free Cleansers

Sulfate-free cleansers have become increasingly popular. Sulfates — such as sodium lauryl sulfate and sodium laureth sulfate — are effective cleansers and produce rich foam, but they can be irritating for some skin types.

Sulfate-free cleansers replace sulfated surfactants with alternatives such as glucosides, sarcosinates, sulfosuccinates and isethionates. These can be milder, but they behave differently. They may foam less, feel different on the skin and require different thickening and preservation strategies.

Formulating a sulfate-free cleanser that performs as well as a sulfate-based one is a technical challenge. It requires careful surfactant selection, the right thickener system and a preservation strategy that works with the chosen surfactants.

Natural and Plant-Derived Surfactants

Natural and plant-derived surfactants are derived from renewable sources such as coconut, sugar and palm. Examples include coco-glucoside, lauryl glucoside, sodium cocoyl glycinate and sodium cocoyl isethionate.

These surfactants can be milder and more sustainable, but they come with formulation challenges. They may foam less, feel different and be more difficult to thicken. They may also vary between batches due to natural raw material variation. If you are pursuing natural or organic certification, you need to check which surfactants are permitted by the certifying body.

Gentle Daily Cleansers

Gentle daily cleansers are the most common cleanser category. They focus on mild surfactant systems that clean effectively without disrupting the skin barrier. Common surfactant choices include sodium lauroyl sarcosinate, cocamidopropyl betaine and glucosides.

The challenge with gentle cleansers is achieving effective cleansing without stripping. The surfactant concentration needs to be high enough to remove dirt and oil, but low enough to avoid irritation. The pH should be close to the skin's natural pH (around 5.0–5.5). Soothing ingredients such as glycerin, panthenol or allantoin can help improve skin comfort.

Deep Cleansing Face Washes

Deep cleansing face washes target oily, acne-prone and congested skin. They use stronger surfactant systems, often based on sodium laureth sulfate or sodium lauryl sulfoacetate, combined with amphoteric surfactants for foam and mildness.

These cleansers may include additional ingredients to address specific concerns. Salicylic acid can help exfoliate inside pores. Niacinamide can help regulate oil production. Zinc can help soothe inflammation. Charcoal or clay can help absorb excess oil.

The challenge is balancing cleansing strength with skin compatibility. A cleanser that is too strong can trigger reactive oil production, making oily skin worse. A cleanser that is too mild may not remove excess oil effectively.

Moisturising Cream Cleansers

Moisturising cream cleansers are designed for dry, sensitive and mature skin. They combine mild surfactants with emollients and humectants, creating a lotion-like texture that feels creamy and comfortable on the skin.

Common emollients include glycerin, shea butter, jojoba oil and squalane. Common humectants include glycerin, hyaluronic acid and panthenol. These ingredients help maintain skin comfort during and after cleansing.

Cream cleansers are often low-foaming or non-foaming. They may be designed to be rinsed off or wiped off with a cotton pad or soft cloth. The formulation needs to balance cleansing performance with skin-conditioning benefits.

Anti-Aging Cleansers

Anti-aging cleansers target fine lines, uneven texture and loss of firmness. They may include gentle exfoliating agents such as alpha hydroxy acids (AHAs) or polyhydroxy acids (PHAs), or skin-conditioning ingredients such as peptides or antioxidants.

The formulation strategy should balance active delivery with mildness and barrier support. Exfoliating cleansers should not be used daily by all skin types, so the formulation and marketing claims need to be aligned with realistic usage patterns.

Blemish-Prone Skin Cleansers

Cleansers for blemish-prone skin require careful consideration of the surfactant system, active ingredients, pH and overall formulation profile. Common actives include salicylic acid, niacinamide, zinc, tea tree oil and azelaic acid.

The surfactant system should be effective at removing excess oil without over-stripping. The pH is important for salicylic acid, which requires a low pH to be effective. Preservation should be effective without being irritating. The overall formulation should be non-comedogenic.

Natural and Botanical Cleansers

Natural and botanical cleansers use plant-derived surfactants, botanical extracts and natural preservation strategies. The challenge is that natural ingredients can be less stable, more variable between batches and more difficult to preserve than synthetic alternatives.

Plant-derived surfactants such as coco-glucoside and lauryl glucoside can be milder but may foam less. Natural preservatives such as sodium benzoate and potassium sorbate are often less effective than synthetic preservatives and may require combination with other preservation strategies. If you are pursuing natural or organic positioning, decide early which standard or certification you will follow, because that decision affects which ingredients are allowed from the very first trial.

Quick answer: Stability testing stores cleanser samples under controlled conditions — room temperature, elevated temperature, refrigeration, freeze-thaw cycles and light exposure — and evaluates appearance, colour, odour, pH, viscosity, foam quality and physical stability over time. Accelerated results give early signals, but real-time data remains the reference for shelf life.

Stability Testing Protocols for Cleansers

A cleanser that looks perfect on the day it is made can separate, discolour or lose foam quality after three months in a warm bathroom. Stability testing is how you find out before your customer does.

A typical stability programme for a cleanser includes:

  • Room temperature storage — the reference condition. Samples stored at 25°C ± 2°C and evaluated at intervals (0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 12, 24 months).
  • Accelerated storage — samples stored at 40°C ± 2°C and 75% relative humidity for 1–3 months. Provides early signals of potential instability.
  • Refrigeration — samples stored at 4°C ± 2°C. Checks whether cold storage causes crystallisation, phase separation or other changes.
  • Freeze-thaw cycling — samples cycled between -10°C and 25°C for multiple cycles. Simulates shipping and storage temperature fluctuations.
  • Light exposure — samples exposed to controlled light to evaluate photostability, especially important for products with natural extracts or sensitive ingredients.

At each evaluation point, parameters such as appearance, colour, odour, pH, viscosity, foam quality, foam stability and physical stability are assessed. Samples should be stored in the real final packaging, because packaging interactions can affect stability.

Preservation and Microbiological Considerations

Water-containing cleansers require appropriate microbial control and preservation. Preservation strategy should be considered alongside formulation composition, pH, surfactant type, packaging, manufacturing and intended use.

Cleansers can be particularly challenging to preserve because surfactants can interact with preservatives, reducing their effectiveness. Some surfactant-based cleansers are difficult to preserve and may require careful selection of preservative systems.

Preservative efficacy testing — also called challenge testing — evaluates whether a preservative system can prevent microbial growth over time. This involves inoculating the product with known microorganisms and monitoring their survival. Common preservative systems for cleansers include phenoxyethanol, sodium benzoate, potassium sorbate, methylparaben, propylparaben and blends of these.

Packaging Compatibility for Cleansers

Packaging is often considered too late in the development process. For certain cleanser concepts, packaging can have an important relationship with product stability and consumer experience. Considerations may include air exposure, container compatibility, pump or flip-top performance, product dispensing, material compatibility and consumer handling.

A technically sound formulation still needs packaging that is appropriate for the product. For example, a foaming cleanser requires a pump that creates the right foam texture. A cream cleanser may need a tube that dispenses evenly. A gel cleanser may need a flip-top bottle that controls flow.

Beyond the Lab

Facial Cleanser Product Development From Concept to Manufacturing

Facial cleanser product development extends beyond laboratory formulation. A commercially prepared product may require support across several stages.

Quick answer: Facial cleanser product development follows a pathway from concept and surfactant strategy through formulation, prototype development, evaluation, stability and testing, packaging considerations and manufacturing scale-up. At the development stage, the formulation also needs to be considered from a manufacturing perspective.

A formula that works in a small laboratory batch may require further optimisation before larger-scale production. Mixing conditions, processing order, raw material handling, equipment and manufacturing parameters can influence the final product. This is where formulation and product development need to work together.

Manufacturing Scale-Up for Cleansers

Scale-up is the step of moving a formula from a small lab batch to commercial production. Batch size, mixing equipment, heating and cooling rates and addition order can all change the finished product.

In the lab, a formulator might use a magnetic stirrer or a small homogeniser. In production, a large mixing tank with a different impeller design creates different flow patterns. A lab batch may heat and cool quickly; a production batch takes much longer. These differences affect the final texture, foam quality, viscosity and stability.

Pilot batches test the scaled-up process before full commercial production. These batches identify potential issues and allow adjustments before committing to large-scale manufacturing. Documentation of process parameters — temperature, mixing speed, addition rate, holding time — ensures the product can be reproduced consistently.

Technology Transfer

Technology transfer turns lab methods into factory instructions. The formulator prepares a detailed manufacturing procedure covering ingredient weights, phase order, mixing conditions, pH adjustment, preservation addition and packaging instructions.

Clear documentation is essential. The manufacturer needs to understand not just what to do, but why each step matters. For example, if a surfactant is sensitive to high shear, the manufacturer needs to know the maximum mixing speed. If a preservative needs to be added at a specific temperature, that needs to be documented.

Working With Contract Manufacturers

Choosing the right manufacturer for your cleanser depends on product category, production scale, equipment, capabilities, minimum order quantities and technical requirements. A formulation consultant can help you evaluate manufacturing options from a product and technical perspective.

Consider the manufacturer's minimum order quantity (MOQ). A high MOQ may not suit a new brand with limited initial demand. A very low MOQ may not have the capacity to scale up later. Communication is key — clear specifications, detailed batch records and regular updates ensure the product meets expectations.

Indian Manufacturing Considerations

India has a growing contract manufacturing sector for skincare products. Many manufacturers specialise in cleansers, face washes and other rinse-off products. But capabilities vary widely. Some manufacturers are equipped for large-volume production but have limited formulation expertise. Others offer both formulation and manufacturing services.

When evaluating manufacturers in India, consider their experience with your product category, their quality systems, their certifications, their minimum order quantities and their ability to scale. A formulation consultant can help you assess these factors and ensure that your formula is compatible with the manufacturer's equipment and processes.

Market Readiness

Regulatory Considerations for Facial Cleansers

Regulatory requirements vary by market and should be considered during development, not only after formulation is complete.

Quick answer: Cosmetic regulations depend on the country, product category, ingredients, claims and how the product is sold. Plan for them during formulation, because a restricted ingredient or an over-stated claim found late can force expensive changes. In India, facial cleansers fall under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and the Cosmetics Rules, 2020.

India: CDSCO and the Cosmetics Rules, 2020

Facial cleansers marketed in India fall under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and Rules, 1945, administered by the Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO) and state FDAs[reference:0]. As cosmetic products, they require compliance with the Cosmetics Rules, 2020 (Schedule S), which mandate that all ingredients be listed on the label in descending order, that product safety be substantiated by the manufacturer, and that heavy metals limits (lead, arsenic, mercury) be within prescribed thresholds[reference:1].

The Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) also has a product standard for facial cleansers (IS 4707:2022), covering pH range (optimum 5.0–7.0 for gentle formulations), microbiological limits, and stability testing. While BIS certification is voluntary, many large retailers and e-commerce platforms now require it for listing[reference:2].

Claim substantiation is a growing regulatory focus. Products labelled "hydrating" or "moisturizing" must have clinical or instrumental evidence of efficacy under the 2020 guidelines. Brands making dermatologist-recommended claims face scrutiny from state FDAs and the Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI)[reference:3].

Imported products must be registered under the Cosmetics Import Procedures (Form 42) and submit a certificate of manufacture and free sale from the country of origin[reference:4]. Under Rule 12(1) of the Cosmetics Rules, 2020, no facial cleanser or face wash may be imported into India unless the product is registered by the Central Licensing Authority[reference:5].

International Markets

In the European Union, cosmetics are regulated under Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009. This requires a Cosmetic Product Safety Report (CPSR), a Product Information File (PIF), and notification to the Cosmetic Products Notification Portal (CPNP). Ingredient restrictions, labelling requirements and claims rules differ from India.

In the United States, cosmetics are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. The FDA does not require pre-market approval for cosmetics, but products must be safe and properly labelled. Cleansers that make drug-like claims (such as treating acne) may be regulated as drugs.

In the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, cosmetics are regulated by the Gulf Standardisation Organisation (GSO). Products must comply with GSO standards for ingredients, labelling and safety. In Southeast Asia, each country has its own regulatory framework, though ASEAN has harmonised cosmetic regulations to some extent.

If you are developing a cleanser for multiple markets, it is more efficient to identify your target markets early and formulate with the most restrictive requirements in mind. Changing a formula after it has been finalised is more expensive than designing for compliance from the start.

Claims and Positioning

A cosmetic cleanser generally cleanses, perfumes, protects, changes appearance or keeps the body in good condition. Wording that suggests treating or preventing disease usually pushes a product out of the cosmetic category and into drug regulation.

Common cosmetic claims for cleansers include "gentle," "hydrating," "purifying," "oil-control," "soothing," "non-comedogenic," "dermatologically tested" and "sulfate-free." Each of these claims has specific implications and may require supporting evidence.

For example, "non-comedogenic" suggests that the product does not clog pores. Supporting this claim may require clinical testing or a review of the ingredient list. "Dermatologically tested" suggests that the product has been tested under dermatological control, which may require documentation.

Claims should be developed in parallel with the formulation. If the formula cannot support the claim, either the formula or the claim needs to change. Addressing this early prevents disappointment and rework.

Frequently Asked Questions About Facial Cleanser Formulation

Facial cleanser formulation is the process of designing a skincare product that removes dirt, oil, makeup and impurities from the facial skin without causing unnecessary dryness or irritation. It involves decisions about surfactant selection, cleansing performance, foam profile, pH, viscosity, preservation, sensory experience and packaging compatibility.

In practice, the terms are often used interchangeably. Technically, a face wash is typically a foaming or gel-based cleanser, while a facial cleanser can include cream, lotion, oil, micellar water and foam formats. The formulation logic differs by format, not by the name on the label.

Common surfactants include anionic surfactants (sodium lauroyl sarcosinate, sodium laureth sulfate, sodium lauryl sulfoacetate), amphoteric surfactants (cocamidopropyl betaine), nonionic surfactants (decyl glucoside, PEG-7 glyceryl cocoate) and cationic surfactants for conditioning cleansers. The choice depends on the desired cleansing strength, foam profile, mildness and skin compatibility.

Gentle cleansers use mild surfactant systems, avoid harsh sulfates at high concentrations, maintain a skin-friendly pH (typically 5.0–6.0), and include soothing or moisturising ingredients such as glycerin, panthenol or botanical extracts. Testing with real consumers is essential to confirm mildness.

The ideal pH depends on the formulation type. Gentle cleansers typically aim for a pH close to the skin's natural pH (around 5.0–5.5). Soap-based cleansers tend to have higher pH (9.0–10.0) and can be more stripping. The pH also affects preservative efficacy and ingredient stability, so it should be determined during formulation development.

Stability testing involves storing samples under controlled conditions (room temperature, elevated temperature, refrigeration, freeze-thaw cycles) and evaluating appearance, colour, odour, pH, viscosity, foam quality and physical stability over time. Accelerated testing provides early signals, but real-time data remains the reference for shelf life.

Water-containing cleansers require appropriate preservation to control microbial growth. Some surfactant-based cleansers are difficult to preserve and may require careful selection of preservative systems. Preservative efficacy testing (challenge testing) confirms whether the chosen system works for the specific formula.

Packaging depends on the product format. Pumps and flip-top bottles suit gels and liquids. Tubes suit creams and lotions. Airless pumps protect sensitive ingredients. Jars suit thicker creams but expose the formula to contamination with each use. The choice affects stability, usability and perceived value.
