Sunscreen Formulation & Product Development

Sunscreen is one of the most technically demanding categories in cosmetic formulation. It requires the right balance of UV protection, photostability, sensory experience, water resistance, preservation and regulatory compliance. Get one element wrong, and the product either fails in testing or fails with consumers.

Whether you are developing a daily-use SPF 50 sunscreen, a mineral sunscreen for sensitive skin, a water-resistant sports formula or a tinted hybrid product, professional formulation support helps turn the concept into a product that protects effectively and feels good enough to use every day.

What Is Sunscreen Formulation?

Quick answer: Sunscreen formulation is the process of designing a topical product that protects the skin from ultraviolet (UV) radiation. It involves selecting UV filters, designing the delivery system (emulsion, gel, spray, stick), optimising SPF and UVA protection, ensuring photostability, achieving the desired sensory profile, and meeting the regulatory requirements of the target market.

Sunscreen is not just another moisturiser with a few UV filters added. It is a highly technical product category with its own chemistry, its own testing requirements and its own regulatory landscape. A sunscreen that looks good on paper can fail in testing because the filters are not photostable, because the film does not spread evenly, or because the emulsifier system breaks down under UV exposure.

And even if it passes every test, it can still fail commercially if consumers do not want to use it. Sunscreens that feel greasy, leave a white cast, pill under makeup or sting the eyes rarely get reapplied as often as they should. Since SPF ratings are based on applying 2 mg/cm² — a much thicker layer than most people use — sensory acceptability is not a luxury. It is a functional requirement.

This is why professional sunscreen formulation matters. It is not simply about mixing UV filters into a base. It is about designing a complete system where protection, stability, sensory experience and regulatory compliance all work together. If you want to see how sunscreen formulation fits into the broader picture of cosmetic product development, read our detailed guide on cosmetic product development.

Why Sunscreen Formulation Is Different From Moisturiser or Serum Formulation

A serum, a cream and a sunscreen are all cosmetic products. But they are formulated according to very different principles.

A serum is a leave-on product designed to deliver active ingredients. Formulation focuses on solubility, pH, absorption, sensory feel and long-term stability. A serum that feels sticky or oxidises is a failed serum.

A moisturiser is also a leave-on product. Its job is to moisturise, protect and support the skin barrier. Formulation focuses on the oil phase, emulsifier system, thickeners and emollients. Texture and sensory feel are critical.

A sunscreen is a leave-on product with a very specific functional requirement: it must protect against UV radiation. That requirement changes everything. The formula must contain enough UV filters to achieve the claimed SPF. The filters must remain stable under UV exposure. The film must spread evenly on the skin. The product must resist water and sweat if it makes that claim. And it must do all this while still being pleasant enough to use every day.

Who Needs Professional Sunscreen Formulation?

Professional sunscreen formulation is relevant for anyone who wants a sunscreen that works — not just a sunscreen that exists. That includes:

  • New skincare brands developing a hero SPF product from scratch.
  • Established brands reformulating an existing sunscreen or launching a new one.
  • Dermatologists and aesthetic clinics creating signature post-procedure or daily sun protection.
  • Private label businesses seeking customisation beyond a generic base.
  • Cosmetic manufacturers needing formulation support for sun care client projects.
  • Entrepreneurs developing a differentiated sun care product for a specific consumer need.

The development approach adapts to the stage of the project, from an early concept to reformulation or preparation for manufacturing.

The Unique Challenges of Sunscreen Formulation

Sunscreen formulators face challenges that do not exist in other cosmetic categories.

SPF vs sensory feel. Higher SPF typically requires more UV filters, which can make the product feel heavier, greasier or more tacky. Finding the balance between protection and comfort is the central challenge.

Photostability. Some UV filters, such as avobenzone, degrade under UV exposure unless stabilised. A sunscreen that loses protection after 30 minutes in the sun is not doing its job.

White cast. Mineral filters like zinc oxide and titanium dioxide can leave a white or chalky appearance on the skin, especially on darker skin tones. Modern dispersion and surface treatment technologies have improved this, but it remains a formulation challenge.

Water resistance. Claims of water resistance require specific testing and formulation strategies. Film-forming polymers, water-repellent ingredients and careful emulsifier selection all play a role.

Preservation. Water-containing sunscreens are susceptible to microbial growth, but UV filters and other ingredients can interact with preservatives. Preservation can be more difficult than in other product categories.

Regulatory complexity. Approved UV filters, maximum concentrations, testing methods and labelling requirements differ between markets. A formula approved in the EU may not be approved in the US, and vice versa. This is perhaps the biggest challenge for brands planning international distribution.

Custom Development

Custom Sunscreen Formulation

Every sun care brand has a different product objective. A startup may want a simple daily SPF 50. An established brand may need a sophisticated hybrid formula with specific sensory and water-resistance requirements.

Quick answer: Custom sunscreen formulation develops the product around your specific concept, target consumer, SPF requirement, sensory goals, water resistance needs, price segment, packaging format and market requirements. It does not force your idea into a generic base.

Custom formulation starts with your brief. The formulator then builds the sunscreen around what you actually want the product to do. Depending on the intended product, formulation development may consider:

  • Target SPF and UVA protection level — SPF 30, SPF 50, SPF 50+, PA+++, PA++++.
  • Skin type and consumer profile — oily, dry, combination, sensitive, acne-prone, mature, darker skin tones.
  • Desired sensory experience — lightweight, non-greasy, matte, dewy, invisible, tinted.
  • Water resistance requirements — no claim, 40-minute, 80-minute, very water-resistant.
  • Filter preferences — chemical, mineral, hybrid, reef-safe, specific filter exclusions.
  • Product format — cream, lotion, gel, spray, stick, fluid, milk.
  • Intended price segment — mass, masstige, prestige, luxury.
  • Packaging format — tube, pump, airless, spray, stick, sachet.
  • Target market and regulatory considerations — India, EU, US, Australia, GCC, Southeast Asia.

The result is a formulation strategy designed around the specific product rather than a one-size-fits-all sunscreen. This is the difference between a product that stands out and a product that blends into a crowded shelf.

Custom Formulation vs Ready-Made Sunscreen Base

A ready-made sunscreen base from a supplier is a fast way to launch. But it comes with limited control and limited differentiation. You are essentially using a formula that other brands may also be using, with minor modifications.

Custom formulation works the other way round. You begin with your customer, your positioning and your goals. The formulation is built or adapted around them. You also come away understanding why each choice was made, which helps every time you launch, reformulate or talk to a manufacturer.

Neither route is wrong. Some brands start with a ready-made base to test the market and move to custom once they understand their customers. Others invest in a custom hero sunscreen from day one because uniqueness is the whole point of the brand. The important thing is to choose knowingly.

Quick answer: Sunscreen formulation development follows six stages: concept and market definition, UV filter selection, delivery system design, SPF and UVA optimisation, photostability and water resistance, and testing and regulatory compliance. Each stage depends on the one before it.

1. Product Concept and Market Definition

The first step is defining what the sunscreen is expected to achieve and where it will be sold. A daily-use SPF 50 for Indian consumers has different requirements from a water-resistant sports sunscreen for the Australian market or a mineral SPF 30 for sensitive skin in Europe.

The concept should specify the target SPF, UVA protection level (PA or critical wavelength), water resistance claim, sensory goals, target consumer, price segment and target markets. This information determines which UV filters are available, which testing methods apply and what claims can be made.

Here is a practical tip: check the approved UV filter list of your target market before you start formulating. If a key filter is not approved in that market, you need to know that before you build the formula around it.

2. UV Filter Selection

UV filters are the heart of any sunscreen. They are the ingredients that absorb, reflect or scatter UV radiation. But not all UV filters are approved in all markets, and not all filters work well together.

UV filters are broadly divided into two categories:

  • Organic (chemical) filters — absorb UV radiation and convert it into heat. Examples include avobenzone, octocrylene, octisalate, homosalate, oxybenzone, bemotrizinol and ethylhexyl triazone.
  • Inorganic (mineral) filters — primarily reflect, scatter and absorb UV radiation. Examples include zinc oxide and titanium dioxide.

Many modern sunscreens use hybrid systems combining both types for better protection, sensory feel and photostability. For example, placing chemical filters in the internal phase and mineral filters in the external phase can maximise SPF synergy and prevent interactions such as the yellowing that occurs when avobenzone meets titanium dioxide.

The filter selection also depends on the desired SPF and UVA protection. Achieving SPF 50 typically requires a combination of filters at specific concentrations, plus SPF boosters and film-forming agents that improve the uniformity of the protective film.

For a broader look at how filter selection fits into the full cosmetic development picture, see our cosmetic product development guide.

3. Delivery System Design

Different sun care concepts require different formulation structures. A sunscreen may be developed as a:

  • Lotion or milk — lightweight, easy to spread, suitable for daily use and large surface areas.
  • Cream — richer, more moisturising, suitable for dry skin and facial use.
  • Gel — lightweight, cooling, suitable for oily skin and humid climates. Often alcohol-based.
  • Spray — convenient for reapplication, suitable for body and outdoor use. Requires careful formulation for even coverage.
  • Stick — convenient for targeted application, especially for the face, ears and nose. Anhydrous or low-water formulas.
  • Fluid — very lightweight, fast-absorbing, often used for facial sunscreens. Can be water-based or silicone-based.
  • Anhydrous system — oil-based or silicone-based. No water, no preservative needed, excellent water resistance.

The choice depends on the skin type, usage occasion, sensory preferences and technical requirements. A daily facial sunscreen might be a lightweight fluid. A body sunscreen for the beach might be a lotion or spray. A sports sunscreen might be a stick or a very water-resistant lotion.

4. SPF and UVA Optimization

Achieving the target SPF is not simply a matter of adding more UV filters. SPF depends on the concentration and type of filters, the uniformity of the film on the skin, the interaction between filters, and the presence of SPF boosters.

SPF boosters are ingredients that improve the efficiency of UV filters without themselves being UV filters. They work by improving film formation, reducing the amount of filter needed, or enhancing the absorption of UV radiation. Examples include certain polymers, esters and silicone derivatives.

UVA protection is equally important. Broad-spectrum protection requires a critical wavelength of at least 370 nm (EU standard) or a UVA-PF to SPF ratio of at least 1:3 (US standard). Achieving this typically requires a combination of UVA filters such as avobenzone, bemotrizinol, zinc oxide or titanium dioxide.

5. Photostability and Water Resistance

Photostability is the ability of a sunscreen to maintain its UV protection when exposed to sunlight. Some UV filters, such as avobenzone, degrade under UV exposure unless stabilised with other ingredients. Photostabilisers such as octocrylene, bemotrizinol and certain polymers help maintain protection throughout sun exposure.

Water resistance is tested by measuring SPF before and after water immersion. Products claiming water resistance must meet specific retention criteria defined by the testing standard used in that market. Achieving water resistance requires film-forming polymers, water-repellent ingredients and careful emulsifier selection.

6. Testing and Regulatory Compliance

A sunscreen needs to pass a comprehensive testing programme before it can be sold. The exact programme depends on the product type, claims and target market. Testing typically includes:

  • SPF testing — in vivo (ISO 24444) or in vitro (ISO 23675, ISO 23698).
  • UVA testing — critical wavelength or UVA-PF measurement.
  • Water resistance testing — SPF retention after immersion.
  • Photostability testing — SPF retention after UV exposure.
  • Stability testing — physical, chemical and sensory stability over time.
  • Preservative efficacy testing — microbial control confirmation.
  • Safety testing — skin compatibility, eye irritation potential.

Regulatory compliance is also essential. The approved UV filters, maximum concentrations, testing methods and labelling requirements differ between markets. A sunscreen that is compliant in the EU may not be compliant in the US, and vice versa. Planning for the target market from the start prevents expensive reformulation later.

Technical Deep Dive

Understanding UV Filters in Sunscreen Formulation

UV filters are the most important ingredient class in any sunscreen. Understanding how they work, how they interact and how they are regulated is essential to formulating a product that protects effectively.

Quick answer: UV filters are ingredients that protect the skin from ultraviolet radiation by absorbing, reflecting or scattering UV rays. They are divided into organic (chemical) filters and inorganic (mineral) filters. The choice, concentration and combination of filters determine the SPF, UVA protection, photostability and sensory properties of the sunscreen.

Organic (Chemical) UV Filters

Organic UV filters absorb UV radiation and convert it into heat, which is then dissipated from the skin. They are typically organic molecules with conjugated double bonds that absorb specific wavelengths of UV radiation.

Common organic UV filters include:

  • Avobenzone (Butyl Methoxydibenzoylmethane) — a UVA filter. It is effective but photounstable unless stabilised with other ingredients such as octocrylene or bemotrizinol.
  • Octocrylene — a UVB filter with some UVA II protection. It is also a photostabiliser for avobenzone.
  • Octisalate (Ethylhexyl Salicylate) — a UVB filter. It is often used as a co-solvent and to improve water resistance.
  • Homosalate — a UVB filter. It is used in combination with other filters to achieve higher SPF.
  • Oxybenzone (Benzophenone-3) — a UVA and UVB filter. It is effective but has faced regulatory restrictions in some markets due to environmental concerns.
  • Bemotrizinol (Bis-Ethylhexyloxyphenol Methoxyphenyl Triazine) — a broad-spectrum filter. It is highly photostable and often used to stabilise avobenzone.
  • Ethylhexyl Triazone — a UVB filter with high efficacy at low concentrations.
  • Methylene Bis-Benzotriazolyl Tetramethylbutylphenol (MBBT) — a broad-spectrum filter with excellent photostability. It is often used in mineral-hybrid systems.

Inorganic (Mineral) UV Filters

Inorganic UV filters, also called mineral or physical filters, primarily reflect, scatter and absorb UV radiation. They are typically metal oxides that form a protective film on the skin surface.

The two approved inorganic UV filters are:

  • Zinc Oxide — provides broad-spectrum protection covering both UVA and UVB. It is photostable and suitable for sensitive skin.
  • Titanium Dioxide — provides UVB protection with some UVA coverage. It is also photostable and suitable for sensitive skin.

Modern advancements in nanotechnology and surface coatings have improved the sensory profile of mineral sunscreens significantly. Coated particles reduce the white cast, improve dispersion and make the product easier to spread. However, the use of nanoparticles is regulated differently across markets, and some consumers prefer non-nano formulations.

Hybrid Filter Systems

Many modern sunscreens use hybrid systems combining organic and inorganic filters. This approach can offer several advantages:

  • Broad-spectrum coverage — combining filters that cover different UV ranges.
  • Photostability — some filters stabilise others. For example, bemotrizinol stabilises avobenzone.
  • SPF synergy — certain filter combinations provide more protection than the sum of their individual contributions.
  • Sensory improvement — mineral filters can improve the feel of organic filters, and vice versa.
  • Reduced filter load — hybrid systems can achieve the target SPF with lower total filter concentration.

One effective strategy is biphasal distribution: placing chemical filters in the internal phase and mineral filters in the external phase. This maximises SPF synergy and prevents unwanted interactions such as the yellowing that can occur when avobenzone meets titanium dioxide.

Photostability and Filter Interactions

Photostability is the ability of a sunscreen to maintain its UV protection when exposed to sunlight. Some UV filters degrade under UV exposure, reducing the protection over time.

Avobenzone is the most well-known example. It provides excellent UVA protection but degrades significantly when exposed to UV radiation unless stabilised. Common photostabilisers for avobenzone include octocrylene, bemotrizinol and certain polymers.

Filter interactions can also affect stability. For example, avobenzone can interact with titanium dioxide or zinc oxide, causing yellowing and reduced efficacy. This is why biphasal distribution or encapsulation strategies are used in hybrid systems.

Photostability testing is essential for any sunscreen that claims broad-spectrum protection. It involves measuring SPF before and after controlled UV exposure to confirm that the protection is maintained.

Daily Facial Sunscreens

Daily facial sunscreens are the fastest-growing segment in the sun care market. Consumers are increasingly aware of the need for daily UV protection, and they want products that feel like skincare, not like traditional sunscreen.

The formulation challenge is achieving high SPF with a lightweight, non-greasy, fast-absorbing texture that works under makeup. Common approaches include fluid emulsions, silicone-based formulas and gel-cream hybrids. SPF boosters and film-forming agents help achieve the target protection with lower filter concentrations.

Compatibility with other skincare products is also important. A facial sunscreen that pills under moisturiser or makes foundation patchy will not be repurchased. Testing with real consumers is essential.

Water-Resistant Body Sunscreens

Water-resistant body sunscreens are designed for beach, pool and outdoor use. They must maintain their SPF after immersion in water, which requires film-forming polymers, water-repellent ingredients and careful emulsifier selection.

The formulation also needs to spread easily over large areas, absorb reasonably quickly and not feel excessively greasy. Water resistance claims are typically tested at 40 or 80 minutes of immersion, depending on the standard used.

Mineral Sunscreens

Mineral sunscreens use zinc oxide and titanium dioxide as the active UV filters. They are generally considered suitable for sensitive skin, children and consumers who prefer mineral-only protection.

The main formulation challenges are white cast, texture and the high concentrations of mineral filters required for adequate SPF. Advances in surface treatment, dispersion technology and particle size have improved the sensory profile significantly. Tinted mineral sunscreens can also help mask the white cast.

Tinted Sunscreens

Tinted sunscreens combine UV protection with light coverage. They are popular with consumers who want to simplify their morning routine or who prefer a more natural look than foundation.

Iron oxides used in tinted sunscreens can also provide additional protection against visible light (blue light) and infrared radiation, which are increasingly recognised as contributing to skin ageing. The shade range is a key consideration — tinted sunscreens need to suit a wide range of skin tones, especially in markets like India with diverse complexions.

Sports Sunscreens

Sports sunscreens are designed for active lifestyles. They need to be very water-resistant, sweat-resistant and long-wearing. They also need to stay in place during movement and not sting the eyes when sweat drips down the face.

Formulation strategies include high concentrations of film-forming polymers, anhydrous or low-water bases, and quick-drying textures. Stick formats are popular for targeted application to the face, ears and nose.

Baby and Kids Sunscreens

Baby and kids sunscreens require extra-gentle formulations. They are often mineral-based, fragrance-free and tear-free. The formulation must be safe for delicate skin and easy for parents to apply to wriggling children.

Sensory considerations are different for children's products. They should not feel sticky or heavy, and they should be easy to spread. Some products use fun formats like sprays or coloured lotions to encourage use, though these may raise additional regulatory considerations.

Quick answer: Sunscreen testing includes SPF determination (in vivo or in vitro), UVA protection measurement (critical wavelength or UVA-PF), water resistance testing, photostability testing, stability testing, preservation testing and safety testing. The exact programme depends on the product type, claims and target market.

SPF Testing Methods

SPF is the ratio of the UV energy required to produce sunburn on protected skin versus unprotected skin. It can be measured using in vivo or in vitro methods.

In vivo SPF testing (ISO 24444) is the gold standard for label claims. It involves applying the sunscreen to the skin of human volunteers and exposing them to controlled UV radiation. The test measures the minimal erythema dose (MED) on protected and unprotected skin. In vivo testing is time-consuming and expensive, but it remains the reference method for regulatory claims.

In vitro SPF testing (ISO 23675, ISO 23698) uses spectrophotometry to measure the UV absorbance of the sunscreen applied to a substrate (such as PMMA plates). In vitro methods are faster and cheaper than in vivo testing, making them useful for screening and development. ISO 23675 is applicable to emulsions and one-phase formulations, while ISO 23698 measures SPF, UVA-PF and critical wavelength without biological responses.

Most brands use in vitro testing during development to screen prototypes, then confirm the final formula with in vivo testing for the label claim.

UVA Testing Methods

UVA protection is measured using the critical wavelength method or the UVA-PF method.

Critical wavelength is the wavelength at which 90% of the total UV absorbance of the sunscreen is below that wavelength. A critical wavelength of at least 370 nm is required for broad-spectrum claims in the EU and many other markets.

UVA-PF is the UVA protection factor, measured using in vivo or in vitro methods. In the US, the FDA requires a UVA-PF to SPF ratio of at least 1:3 for broad-spectrum claims.

The PA rating system (PA+, PA++, PA+++, PA++++) is commonly used in Asia and is based on the UVA-PF value. PA+ indicates low UVA protection, PA++++ indicates the highest level.

Water Resistance Testing

Water resistance is tested by measuring SPF before and after immersion in water. The standard immersion times are 40 minutes (water-resistant) and 80 minutes (very water-resistant), though the exact requirements vary by market.

The test involves applying the sunscreen, immersing the subject in water for the specified time, then measuring SPF immediately after. The product must retain a certain percentage of its original SPF to make the claim. In some markets, the label must state the SPF after water immersion.

Photostability Testing

Photostability testing measures how well the sunscreen maintains its SPF after exposure to UV radiation. The test involves applying the sunscreen, exposing it to a controlled dose of UV radiation, then measuring SPF. A photostable sunscreen should retain most of its original SPF after exposure.

Photostability is particularly important for sunscreens containing avobenzone, which degrades under UV exposure unless stabilised. Formulators can improve photostability by using photostabilisers, encapsulation or filter combinations.

Stability Testing for Sunscreens

A sunscreen needs to remain stable throughout its shelf life. Stability testing involves storing samples under controlled conditions and evaluating them at regular intervals.

Parameters evaluated during stability testing include appearance, colour, odour, pH, viscosity, texture, SPF retention, UVA retention and physical stability. Samples should be stored in the real final packaging, because packaging interactions can affect stability.

Sunscreens are often stored in hot conditions (beach bags, car glove boxes, outdoor use), so stability testing should account for real-world storage conditions. Elevated temperature testing at 40°C is common.

Preservation and Safety Testing

Water-containing sunscreens require preservation to control microbial growth. Preservation can be challenging because UV filters, emulsifiers and other ingredients can interact with preservatives. Preservative efficacy testing (challenge testing) confirms whether the chosen system works.

Safety testing may include skin compatibility testing, eye irritation testing (especially for facial sunscreens and sprays), and in some cases, human repeat insult patch testing (HRIPT). The exact requirements depend on the product type and target market.

Beyond the Lab

Sunscreen Product Development From Concept to Manufacturing

Sunscreen product development extends beyond laboratory formulation. A commercially prepared product may require support across several stages.

Quick answer: Sunscreen product development follows a pathway from concept and filter strategy through formulation, prototype development, testing, packaging considerations and manufacturing scale-up. At the development stage, the formulation also needs to be considered from a manufacturing perspective.

A formula that works in a small laboratory batch may require further optimisation before larger-scale production. Mixing conditions, processing order, raw material handling, equipment and manufacturing parameters can influence the final product. This is where formulation and product development need to work together.

Manufacturing Scale-Up for Sunscreens

Scale-up is the step of moving a formula from a small lab batch to commercial production. Batch size, mixing equipment, heating and cooling rates and addition order can all change the finished product.

Sunscreen scale-up has particular challenges. UV filters must be evenly dispersed throughout the batch. If the dispersion is not uniform, the SPF will vary from batch to batch and within the same batch. High-shear mixing is often required to achieve uniform dispersion, but excessive shear can damage encapsulated filters or destabilise the emulsion.

Pilot batches test the scaled-up process before full commercial production. These batches identify potential issues and allow adjustments before committing to large-scale manufacturing. Documentation of process parameters — temperature, mixing speed, addition rate, holding time — ensures the product can be reproduced consistently.

Technology Transfer

Technology transfer turns lab methods into factory instructions. The formulator prepares a detailed manufacturing procedure covering ingredient weights, phase order, mixing conditions, pH adjustment, preservation addition and packaging instructions.

Clear documentation is essential. The manufacturer needs to understand not just what to do, but why each step matters. For example, if a UV filter is sensitive to high shear, the manufacturer needs to know the maximum mixing speed. If a photostabiliser needs to be added at a specific temperature, that needs to be documented.

Working With Contract Manufacturers

Choosing the right manufacturer for your sunscreen depends on product category, production scale, equipment, capabilities, minimum order quantities and technical requirements. A formulation consultant can help you evaluate manufacturing options from a product and technical perspective.

Consider the manufacturer's experience with sun care products. Sunscreen manufacturing requires specialised equipment for dispersing UV filters, testing SPF during production and handling the regulatory documentation. Not all cosmetic manufacturers have this capability.

Minimum order quantities are also a consideration. A high MOQ may not suit a new brand with limited initial demand. A very low MOQ may not have the capacity to scale up later. Communication is key — clear specifications, detailed batch records and regular updates ensure the product meets expectations.

For a broader look at how manufacturing and scale-up fit into the complete product development journey, see our full cosmetic product development guide.

Indian Manufacturing Considerations

India has a growing contract manufacturing sector for sun care products. Many manufacturers specialise in sunscreens, lotions and creams. But capabilities vary widely. Some manufacturers are equipped for large-volume production but have limited formulation expertise. Others offer both formulation and manufacturing services.

When evaluating manufacturers in India, consider their experience with sun care, their SPF testing capabilities, their quality systems, their certifications, their minimum order quantities and their ability to scale. Sunscreen manufacturing requires close attention to dispersion quality and batch uniformity, so the manufacturer's equipment and processes matter.

Market Readiness

Regulatory Considerations for Sunscreens

Regulatory requirements vary by market and should be considered during development, not only after formulation is complete.

Quick answer: Sunscreen regulations differ significantly between markets. The approved UV filters, maximum concentrations, testing methods, labelling requirements and claims rules all vary. In India, sunscreens are regulated as cosmetics under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and the Cosmetics Rules, 2020, with SPF and UVA claims requiring substantiation.

India: CDSCO and the Cosmetics Rules, 2020

In India, sunscreens are regulated as cosmetics under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and the Cosmetics Rules, 2020. The Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO) and state FDAs oversee compliance.

Sunscreens imported into India require registration with CDSCO before they can be sold. The registration process requires SPF and UVA test reports, country-of-origin specifications and other documentation. Sunscreen has no specific Indian Standard under the Ninth Schedule, so CDSCO relies heavily on the SPF and UVA test reports and the country-of-origin specification.

A critical shift in 2026 is the mandatory alignment with Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) quality control orders for high-risk categories like sunscreens. Labelling requirements have also been refined to distinguish between "Use Before" and "Date of Expiry" to enhance consumer safety.

Manufacturing premises must comply with Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP), and products must meet the safety and labelling requirements specified in the Cosmetics Rules. SPF and UVA claims must be substantiated with appropriate test reports from accredited laboratories.

European Union

In the EU, sunscreens are regulated as cosmetics under Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009. The regulation specifies approved UV filters in Annex VI, with maximum concentrations for each. Products must have a Cosmetic Product Safety Report (CPSR), a Product Information File (PIF), and notification to the Cosmetic Products Notification Portal (CPNP).

Broad-spectrum claims require a critical wavelength of at least 370 nm. UVA protection must be at least one-third of the SPF. Labelling requirements are strict, and claims must be substantiated.

United States

In the US, sunscreens are regulated as over-the-counter (OTC) drugs by the FDA under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. The FDA OTC monograph specifies approved UV filters and maximum concentrations. The approved filter list is more limited than in the EU, which restricts formulation options.

Broad-spectrum claims require a UVA-PF to SPF ratio of at least 1:3. Water resistance claims require specific testing. The FDA has a proposed rule that would add bemotrizinol to the monograph, which would expand formulation options for US brands.

Australia

In Australia, sunscreens are regulated as therapeutic goods by the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA). They must be listed or registered on the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG). The TGA has its own approved filter list, which is similar to but not identical to the EU list.

Other Markets

In Japan, sunscreens are regulated under the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Act. The approved filter list is similar to the EU but includes some unique filters. In GCC countries, GSO standards align closely with EU regulations. In Southeast Asia, ASEAN has harmonised cosmetic regulations to some extent, but each country has its own requirements.

If you are developing a sunscreen for multiple markets, it is more efficient to identify your target markets early and formulate with the most restrictive requirements in mind. Changing a formula after it has been finalised is more expensive than designing for compliance from the start.

Claims and Labelling

Sunscreen claims are heavily regulated because consumers rely on them for protection. Common claims include SPF level (SPF 30, SPF 50, SPF 50+), broad-spectrum, water-resistant, very water-resistant, UVA protection (PA+, PA++, etc.) and dermatologically tested.

Each claim has specific requirements. For example, SPF 50+ is allowed in some markets but not others. Water resistance claims require specific testing and must state the SPF after immersion. Broad-spectrum claims require a critical wavelength of at least 370 nm or a UVA-PF to SPF ratio of at least 1:3.

Claims should be developed in parallel with the formulation. If the formula cannot support the claim, either the formula or the claim needs to change. Addressing this early prevents disappointment and rework.

This section is general information, not legal advice. Always confirm current requirements with the relevant authority or a qualified regulatory professional.

Frequently Asked Questions About Sunscreen Formulation

Sunscreen formulation is the process of designing a topical product that protects the skin from ultraviolet (UV) radiation. It involves selecting UV filters, designing the delivery system (emulsion, gel, spray, stick), optimising SPF and UVA protection, ensuring photostability, achieving the desired sensory profile, and meeting the regulatory requirements of the target market.

Chemical (organic) sunscreens use UV filters that absorb UV radiation and convert it into heat. Mineral (inorganic) sunscreens use zinc oxide or titanium dioxide, which primarily reflect, scatter and absorb UV radiation. Many modern sunscreens use hybrid systems combining both types for better protection, sensory feel and photostability.

SPF indicates the level of protection against UVB rays. SPF 30 blocks approximately 97% of UVB rays, while SPF 50 blocks approximately 98%. Most regulatory bodies recommend a minimum of SPF 30 for daily use. The choice depends on the target market, consumer expectations, intended use (daily vs. outdoor) and regulatory claims allowed in that market.

Broad-spectrum protection means the sunscreen protects against both UVA and UVB radiation. UVA causes premature ageing and deeper skin damage, while UVB causes sunburn. A broad-spectrum sunscreen must meet specific UVA protection criteria, such as a critical wavelength of at least 370 nm or a UVA-PF to SPF ratio of at least 1:3, depending on the regulatory framework.

SPF is tested using in vivo methods on human volunteers (ISO 24444) or in vitro methods using spectrophotometry (ISO 23675, ISO 23698). In vivo testing remains the gold standard for label claims, while in vitro methods are used for screening and development. Both methods measure the ratio of UV energy required to produce sunburn on protected skin versus unprotected skin.

Water resistance indicates how well a sunscreen maintains its SPF after immersion in water. It is tested by measuring SPF before and after water immersion (typically 40 or 80 minutes, depending on the claim). Products claiming water resistance must meet specific retention criteria defined by the testing standard used in that market.

Key challenges include achieving high SPF with acceptable sensory feel, ensuring photostability of UV filters, preventing white cast with mineral filters, maintaining water resistance, ensuring compatibility between filters, preserving the formula effectively, and meeting the different regulatory requirements of each market. Sensory acceptability is critical because consumers must want to apply enough product for the labelled SPF to be achieved.

Photostability refers to the ability of a sunscreen to maintain its UV protection when exposed to sunlight. Some UV filters, such as avobenzone, degrade under UV exposure unless stabilised with other ingredients. Formulators use photostabilisers, encapsulation and filter combinations to maintain protection throughout sun exposure.

Yes. Mineral sunscreens use zinc oxide and titanium dioxide as UV filters. They are generally considered suitable for sensitive skin, but they can be challenging to formulate because of white cast, texture and the high concentrations required for adequate SPF. Advances in surface treatment, dispersion and particle size have improved the sensory profile of mineral sunscreens significantly.

Water-containing sunscreens require preservation to control microbial growth. Preservation can be challenging because UV filters, emulsifiers and other ingredients can interact with preservatives. Preservative efficacy testing (challenge testing) confirms whether the chosen system works. Some sunscreens are formulated as anhydrous or water-resistant systems, which can reduce preservation challenges.

Packaging depends on the product format. Tubes suit creams and lotions. Pumps suit lotions and sprays. Airless pumps protect sensitive formulas. Sticks suit solid formats. Squeeze bottles suit gels and milks. The packaging must protect the formula from light and air, dispense the right amount, and suit consumer usage habits.

Sunscreen development typically takes longer than other cosmetic categories because of the extensive testing required. SPF testing, UVA testing, water resistance testing, stability testing and photostability testing all add calendar time. Plan in months, not weeks, and expect the process to involve multiple prototype rounds before finalising the formula.
