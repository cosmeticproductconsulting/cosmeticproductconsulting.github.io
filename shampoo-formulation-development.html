Shampoo Formulation & Product Development

Shampoo looks like a simple product. Water, surfactants, some conditioning agents, fragrance, a bottle. But the difference between a shampoo that leaves hair clean, soft and manageable and one that leaves it dry, tangled and dull comes down to formulation decisions made long before the product reaches a shelf.

Whether you are developing a gentle daily shampoo, a sulfate-free formula for curly hair, an anti-dandruff treatment or a color-protecting wash, professional formulation support helps turn the concept into a product that cleans effectively, feels pleasant and stays stable.

What Is Shampoo Formulation?

Quick answer: Shampoo formulation is the process of designing a hair cleansing product that removes dirt, oil, product buildup and environmental pollutants from the hair and scalp while maintaining hair manageability, softness and scalp comfort. It involves decisions about surfactant systems, conditioning agents, pH, viscosity, preservation, fragrance and packaging compatibility.

Hair cleansing is something most people do without thinking. Wet the hair, apply shampoo, lather, rinse, repeat. But behind that simple routine sits a complex formulation challenge. The product needs to clean effectively without stripping the hair of its natural oils. It needs to produce a satisfying lather. It needs to leave hair feeling soft and manageable, not squeaky or coated. It needs to be stable for two years. It needs to be preserved against microbial growth. And it needs to do all this at a price that works commercially.

The word "shampoo" covers a wide range of product types. A clarifying shampoo, a moisturising shampoo, an anti-dandruff shampoo, a sulfate-free shampoo, a color-protecting shampoo, a baby shampoo and a dry shampoo are all shampoos. But they are formulated according to very different logic. A clarifying shampoo uses stronger surfactants to remove buildup. A moisturising shampoo uses milder surfactants and conditioning agents. An anti-dandruff shampoo includes active ingredients that target scalp conditions. A sulfate-free shampoo replaces sulfated surfactants with alternatives that behave differently.

Understanding these differences is the starting point of shampoo development. If you want to see how shampoo formulation fits into the broader picture of cosmetic product development, read our detailed guide on cosmetic product development.

Why Shampoo Formulation Is Different From Serum or Sunscreen Formulation

A serum, a sunscreen and a shampoo are all cosmetic products. But they are formulated according to very different principles.

A serum is a leave-on product designed to deliver active ingredients. Formulation focuses on solubility, pH, absorption, sensory feel and long-term stability. A serum that feels sticky or oxidises is a failed serum.

A sunscreen is a leave-on product with a very specific functional requirement: it must protect against UV radiation. Formulation focuses on UV filters, photostability, film formation and SPF testing.

A shampoo is a rinse-off product. Its job is to clean the hair and scalp, then disappear down the drain. Formulation focuses on surfactant systems that clean effectively without stripping. The product does not stay on the hair, so the bar for long-term compatibility is different from a leave-on product. But the immediate after-feel — how the hair feels when wet, how it combs, how it dries — matters enormously.

Shampoo formulation also sits at the intersection of cleaning science and conditioning science. The same product must remove oil and dirt while depositing conditioning agents that make hair soft and manageable. This is a delicate balance. Too much cleansing and the hair feels dry. Too much conditioning and the hair feels greasy or weighed down.

Who Needs Professional Shampoo Formulation?

Professional shampoo formulation is relevant for anyone who wants a shampoo that works — not just a shampoo that exists. That includes:

  • New hair care brands developing a hero shampoo from scratch.
  • Established brands reformulating an existing shampoo or launching a new one.
  • Salons and hair clinics creating signature retail or aftercare shampoos.
  • Private label businesses seeking customisation beyond a generic base.
  • Cosmetic manufacturers needing formulation support for client projects.
  • Entrepreneurs developing a differentiated hair care product for a specific consumer need.

The development approach adapts to the stage of the project, from an early concept to reformulation or preparation for manufacturing.

The Unique Challenges of Shampoo Formulation

Shampoo formulators face challenges that do not exist in other cosmetic categories.

Cleansing vs conditioning. Shampoos are based on anionic surfactants, which are negatively charged. Conditioning agents, such as cationic polymers, are positively charged. They are attracted to each other, which means the conditioning agent can be neutralised by the surfactant before it ever reaches the hair. Getting the balance right is the central challenge.

Foam quality. Consumers judge shampoo by its lather. A shampoo that does not foam well feels like it is not cleaning, even if it is. Foam quality depends on surfactant selection, foam boosters and the presence of oils or conditioning agents that can suppress foam.

Viscosity control. Shampoo needs to be thick enough to stay in the hand but thin enough to spread through the hair. Achieving the right viscosity requires thickeners and salt curve management. Viscosity is also sensitive to temperature and scale-up.

Hair type diversity. A shampoo that works for fine, straight hair may be too heavy for oily hair and too light for curly hair. Formulating for a specific hair type requires understanding how different ingredients interact with different hair textures.

Preservation. Water-containing shampoos are susceptible to microbial growth, but surfactants can interact with preservatives, reducing their effectiveness. Preservation can be more challenging than in other product categories.

Regulatory complexity. Shampoo regulations differ between markets. In India, shampoos must comply with BIS specification IS 4707, which covers pH, detergent concentration, heavy metal limits and microbial limits. Claim substantiation is also increasingly enforced.

Custom Development

Custom Shampoo Formulation

Every hair care brand has a different product objective. A startup may want a simple gentle cleanser. An established brand may need a sophisticated formula with specific conditioning and sensory requirements.

Quick answer: Custom shampoo formulation develops the product around your specific concept, target consumer, hair type, sensory goals, price segment, packaging format and market requirements. It does not force your idea into a generic base.

Custom formulation starts with your brief. The formulator then builds the shampoo around what you actually want the product to do. Depending on the intended product, formulation development may consider:

  • Hair type and consumer profile — straight, wavy, curly, coily, fine, medium, thick, oily, dry, damaged, color-treated.
  • Desired cleansing performance — gentle daily cleanse, deep clean, clarifying, moisturising, volumising.
  • Target texture and sensory experience — rich lather, creamy lather, low-foam, silky, lightweight, cooling.
  • Ingredient preferences — natural, botanical, sulfate-free, silicone-free, fragrance-free, vegan.
  • Intended price segment — mass, masstige, prestige, luxury.
  • Packaging format — bottle with flip-top cap, pump, tube, sachet, airless.
  • Manufacturing requirements — batch size, equipment, MOQ, scalability.
  • Target market and regulatory considerations — India, EU, US, GCC, Southeast Asia.

The result is a formulation strategy designed around the specific product rather than a one-size-fits-all shampoo. This is the difference between a product that stands out and a product that blends into a crowded shelf.

Custom Formulation vs Ready-Made Shampoo Base

A ready-made shampoo base from a supplier is a fast way to launch. But it comes with limited control and limited differentiation. You are essentially using a formula that other brands may also be using, with minor modifications.

Custom formulation works the other way round. You begin with your customer, your positioning and your goals. The formulation is built or adapted around them. You also come away understanding why each choice was made, which helps every time you launch, reformulate or talk to a manufacturer.

Neither route is wrong. Some brands start with a ready-made base to test the market and move to custom once they understand their customers. Others invest in a custom hero shampoo from day one because uniqueness is the whole point of the brand. The important thing is to choose knowingly.

Quick answer: Shampoo formulation development follows six stages: product concept and hair type definition, surfactant system selection, conditioning and functional agent selection, pH and viscosity development, preservation and product protection, and stability and compatibility evaluation. Each stage depends on the one before it.

1. Product Concept and Hair Type Definition

The first step is defining what the shampoo is expected to achieve. A product may be positioned around gentle daily cleansing, deep pore cleansing, moisture retention, volume, color protection, scalp soothing or anti-dandruff action.

The concept influences surfactant selection, conditioning agents, product format, sensory profile and development strategy. A gentle daily shampoo for fine hair will look very different from a rich moisturising shampoo for thick, curly hair. The concept should be specific enough to guide formulation decisions.

Here is a practical tip: write the concept as if you are explaining the product to someone who has never used anything like it. If you cannot explain it clearly, the formulator will not be able to build it clearly.

2. Surfactant System Selection

Surfactants are the heart of any shampoo. They are the ingredients that actually remove oil, dirt and product buildup from the hair and scalp. But not all surfactants are the same. Some are harsh. Some are gentle. Some foam richly. Some barely foam at all. Some are easy to preserve. Some are difficult.

Shampoo formulation typically uses a blend of surfactants rather than a single surfactant. The primary surfactant does the heavy lifting of cleansing. The secondary surfactant boosts foam, reduces irritation and improves viscosity.

Common primary surfactants include:

  • Sodium Laureth Sulfate (SLES) — the most widely used primary surfactant. Good cleansing and foaming, lower irritation potential than SLS, good compatibility with other ingredients.
  • Sodium Lauryl Sulfate (SLS) — strong cleansing and rich foam, but higher irritation potential. Often used in clarifying shampoos.
  • Sodium Lauroyl Methyl Isethionate — a mild, sulfate-free surfactant with excellent foam and a clean after-feel.
  • Sodium Cocoyl Isethionate — a very mild, sulfate-free surfactant. Often used in syndet bars and gentle shampoos.
  • Alpha-Olefin Sulfonate (AOS) — good foaming and cleansing, stable across pH ranges, milder than SLS.

Common secondary surfactants include:

  • Cocamidopropyl Betaine — an amphoteric surfactant that boosts foam, reduces irritation from the primary surfactant and improves viscosity. Used at 2–10%.
  • Coco-Glucoside — a nonionic surfactant that adds mildness and a creamy foam. Often used in sulfate-free formulas.
  • Lauryl Glucoside — similar to coco-glucoside, with good foam and mildness.
  • Cocamide MEA or DEA — foam boosters and viscosity builders. Used at 1–3%.

The surfactant system also affects the product's texture, viscosity and rinse feel. Getting this right is the most important technical decision in shampoo formulation.

3. Conditioning and Functional Agents

A shampoo that only cleans is not enough. Consumers expect their hair to feel soft, smooth and manageable after washing. This is where conditioning agents come in.

Conditioning agents work by depositing on the hair surface, reducing friction, smoothing the cuticle and reducing static. Common conditioning agents include:

  • Cationic polymers — polyquaternium-7, polyquaternium-10, guar hydroxypropyltrimonium chloride. These deposit on the negatively charged hair surface, improving wet and dry combability. Used at 0.1–2%.
  • Silicones — dimethicone, amodimethicone, dimethiconol. These provide slip, shine and smoothness. Used at 1–5% in 2-in-1 shampoos.
  • Protein hydrolysates — hydrolysed wheat protein, hydrolysed silk, keratin. These temporarily fill gaps in the hair cuticle and improve strength and manageability.
  • Panthenol (Pro-Vitamin B5) — a humectant that improves moisture retention and adds shine.
  • Fatty alcohols — cetyl alcohol, stearyl alcohol. These are not drying alcohols; they are waxy solids that add emollience and body.

Functional actives may also be included depending on the product concept:

  • Anti-dandruff agents — zinc pyrithione, piroctone olamine, selenium sulfide, ketoconazole, salicylic acid.
  • Color protection agents — UV filters, antioxidants, pH buffers.
  • Scalp soothing agents — aloe vera, chamomile, tea tree oil, menthol.
  • Volumising agents — hydrolysed proteins, certain polymers that coat the hair shaft.

4. pH, Viscosity and Sensory Development

pH is a critical parameter in shampoo formulation. It affects scalp compatibility, cuticle smoothness, preservative efficacy and product stability.

The optimal pH for shampoo is typically 4.5 to 6.0, which is close to the natural pH of the scalp and hair. This slightly acidic range helps maintain the scalp's moisture barrier, keeps the hair cuticle smooth and improves shine. A lower pH (more acidic) can improve cuticle smoothness and color retention. A higher pH (more alkaline) can make the hair feel rougher and more prone to tangling.

Viscosity is equally important from a consumer perspective. Shampoo needs to be thick enough to stay in the hand but thin enough to spread through the hair. Viscosity is typically controlled using:

  • Electrolytes — sodium chloride (salt) is the most common viscosity modifier. Adding salt increases viscosity up to a point, then it starts to decrease. This is called the "salt curve."
  • Thickeners — PEG-150 distearate, PEG-120 methyl glucose dioleate, carbomer, xanthan gum.
  • Foam boosters — cocamide MEA, cocamide DEA.

Sensory development considers lather quality (rich, creamy, airy), rinse feel (squeaky, silky, slippery), wet combability, dry combability, softness, shine and static control.

5. Preservation and Product Protection

Products containing water generally require an appropriate preservation strategy to help control microbial contamination. Shampoos can be particularly challenging to preserve because the surfactant system can interact with preservatives, reducing their effectiveness.

Some surfactant-based shampoos are difficult to preserve and may require careful selection of preservative systems. Preservative efficacy testing (challenge testing per ISO 11930) confirms that the system is working.

Common preservative systems for shampoos include:

  • Phenoxyethanol — broad-spectrum, compatible with most surfactants.
  • Sodium Benzoate — effective at low pH, often used in combination with other preservatives.
  • Potassium Sorbate — effective against fungi and yeast, often used with sodium benzoate.
  • Methylisothiazolinone (MIT) — effective at low concentrations, but restricted in some markets for leave-on products.
  • Blends — combinations of preservatives that work together for broad-spectrum protection.

6. Stability and Compatibility Evaluation

A shampoo needs to remain suitable throughout its intended shelf life and storage conditions. Development may therefore involve evaluating characteristics such as appearance, colour, odour, pH, viscosity, foam quality, physical stability and packaging compatibility.

Shampoos are often stored in bathrooms, where temperature and humidity fluctuate. Stability testing should account for real-world storage conditions, not just controlled laboratory conditions. Products containing natural ingredients or sensitive actives may require additional stability considerations.

Technical Deep Dive

Understanding Surfactants in Shampoo Formulation

Surfactants are the most important ingredient class in any shampoo. Understanding how they work is essential to formulating a product that cleans effectively without damaging the hair.

Quick answer: Surfactants are molecules with a water-loving head and an oil-loving tail. They work by reducing the surface tension between water and oil, allowing oil and dirt to be lifted from the hair and scalp and rinsed away. The type, concentration and blend of surfactants determine how a shampoo performs, how it feels and how mild it is on the hair and scalp.

How Surfactants Work on Hair

Hair and scalp produce sebum, an oily substance that helps protect the hair and skin. Dirt, pollution, dead skin cells and product buildup also accumulate on the hair surface. Water alone cannot remove these because oil and water do not mix.

Surfactants solve this problem. Their molecular structure has two ends: a hydrophilic (water-loving) head and a lipophilic (oil-loving) tail. The tail attaches to oil and dirt. The head attaches to water. When the shampoo is rinsed away, the oil and dirt are carried with it.

This is why surfactants are called "surface-active agents." They change the surface properties of water, allowing it to wet and penetrate materials it would normally repel.

Choosing the Right Surfactant System

The surfactant system is not just one ingredient. It is a carefully designed blend. Most shampoos use a combination of surfactants to achieve the desired balance of cleansing strength, foam quality, mildness and cost.

For example, a gentle daily shampoo might combine:

  • Sodium Lauroyl Methyl Isethionate — a mild, sulfate-free primary surfactant with excellent foam.
  • Cocamidopropyl Betaine — an amphoteric surfactant that boosts foam and reduces irritation.
  • Coco-Glucoside — a nonionic surfactant that adds mildness and a creamy foam.

A stronger clarifying shampoo might combine sodium laureth sulfate with cocamidopropyl betaine and a small amount of a nonionic surfactant for foam stability.

Mildness vs Cleansing Strength

There is always a trade-off between mildness and cleansing strength. Stronger surfactants clean more effectively but can strip the hair and irritate the scalp. Milder surfactants are gentler but may not remove heavy buildup or excess oil as efficiently.

The formulator's job is to find the right balance for the intended consumer. A shampoo for daily use should be gentle enough for frequent washing. A clarifying shampoo for product buildup may need stronger cleansing. A shampoo for color-treated hair should be gentle enough to preserve color.

If you are developing a shampoo for the Indian market, where consumers often use hair oil and live in varying climates, the balance between mildness and cleansing strength becomes especially important. Indian hair care routines often involve pre-wash oiling, which means the shampoo needs to remove oil effectively without over-stripping.

Surfactant Interactions and Formulation Challenges

Surfactants do not work in isolation. They interact with other ingredients in the formula, and these interactions can create challenges.

Some surfactants interact with preservatives, reducing their effectiveness. This is why shampoos can be difficult to preserve. Some surfactants interact with thickeners, affecting viscosity. Some surfactants are sensitive to pH changes. Some surfactants can cause cloudiness or separation in the final product.

Understanding these interactions is a key part of professional shampoo formulation. It is also why trial-and-error formulation rarely produces a reliable result. The formulator needs to understand the chemistry of the system, not just mix ingredients together.

Sulfate-Free Shampoos

Sulfate-free shampoos have become increasingly popular. Sulfates — such as sodium lauryl sulfate and sodium laureth sulfate — are effective cleansers and produce rich foam, but they can be irritating for some hair types and scalp conditions.

Sulfate-free shampoos replace sulfated surfactants with alternatives such as glucosides, isethionates, sarcosinates and sulfosuccinates. These can be milder, but they behave differently. They may foam less, feel different on the hair and require different thickening and preservation strategies.

Formulating a sulfate-free shampoo that performs as well as a sulfate-based one is a technical challenge. It requires careful surfactant selection, the right thickener system and a preservation strategy that works with the chosen surfactants.

Natural and Plant-Derived Surfactants

Natural and plant-derived surfactants are derived from renewable sources such as coconut, sugar and palm. Examples include coco-glucoside, lauryl glucoside, sodium cocoyl glycinate and sodium cocoyl isethionate.

These surfactants can be milder and more sustainable, but they come with formulation challenges. They may foam less, feel different and be more difficult to thicken. They may also vary between batches due to natural raw material variation. If you are pursuing natural or organic certification, you need to check which surfactants are permitted by the certifying body.

Gentle Daily Shampoos

Gentle daily shampoos are the most common shampoo category. They focus on mild surfactant systems that clean effectively without disrupting the hair and scalp. Common surfactant choices include sodium lauroyl methyl isethionate, cocamidopropyl betaine and glucosides.

The challenge with gentle shampoos is achieving effective cleansing without stripping. The surfactant concentration needs to be high enough to remove dirt and oil, but low enough to avoid irritation. The pH should be close to the scalp's natural pH (around 5.0–5.5). Conditioning agents such as polyquaternium-10 or guar gum derivatives can improve wet combability without weighing hair down.

Moisturising Shampoos

Moisturising shampoos are designed for dry, damaged or chemically treated hair. They combine mild surfactants with conditioning agents and humectants, creating a richer, creamier lather that leaves hair feeling soft and nourished.

Common conditioning agents include cationic polymers, silicones, protein hydrolysates, panthenol and fatty alcohols. Common humectants include glycerin, hyaluronic acid and panthenol. These ingredients help maintain moisture balance during and after washing.

Moisturising shampoos are often richer and thicker than daily shampoos. They may be low-foaming or produce a creamy foam rather than a rich lather.

Clarifying Shampoos

Clarifying shampoos target product buildup, hard water mineral deposits and excess oil. They use stronger surfactant systems, often based on sodium laureth sulfate or sodium lauryl sulfate, combined with chelating agents such as EDTA or phytic acid.

These shampoos are not designed for daily use. They are typically used once a week or as needed to remove buildup. The formulation needs to balance strong cleansing with hair compatibility to avoid over-stripping.

Color-Protecting Shampoos

Color-protecting shampoos are designed for color-treated hair. They use gentle, low-pH formulas that help preserve color and reduce fading. The pH is typically 4.5 to 5.5, which helps keep the hair cuticle smooth and reduces color loss.

Common ingredients include UV filters to protect against sun damage, antioxidants to reduce oxidation, and conditioning agents to maintain hair softness. Some formulas include color-depositing ingredients to refresh tone between salon visits.

Anti-Dandruff Shampoos

Anti-dandruff shampoos include active ingredients that target scalp flaking, itching and irritation. Common actives include zinc pyrithione, piroctone olamine, selenium sulfide, ketoconazole and salicylic acid.

The formulation strategy should balance active delivery with mildness and scalp compatibility. Anti-dandruff shampoos may be used several times a week, so they should not be overly drying. Preservative selection is also important because some actives can interact with preservatives.

Sulfate-Free Shampoos

Sulfate-free shampoos use non-sulfated surfactants such as glucosides, isethionates, sarcosinates and sulfosuccinates. They are marketed as gentler alternatives to traditional shampoos, particularly for sensitive scalp, color-treated hair and curly hair.

The formulation challenge is achieving good foam and cleansing with surfactants that behave differently from sulfates. Sulfate-free surfactants may foam less, feel different and require different thickening and preservation strategies. Formulating a sulfate-free shampoo that performs well requires careful surfactant selection and testing.

Quick answer: Stability testing stores shampoo samples under controlled conditions — room temperature, elevated temperature, refrigeration, freeze-thaw cycles and light exposure — and evaluates appearance, colour, odour, pH, viscosity, foam quality and physical stability over time. Accelerated results give early signals, but real-time data remains the reference for shelf life.

Stability Testing Protocols for Shampoos

A shampoo that looks perfect on the day it is made can separate, discolour or lose viscosity after three months in a warm bathroom. Stability testing is how you find out before your customer does.

A typical stability programme for a shampoo includes:

  • Room temperature storage — the reference condition. Samples stored at 25°C ± 2°C and evaluated at intervals (0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 12, 24 months).
  • Accelerated storage — samples stored at 40°C ± 2°C and 75% relative humidity for 1–3 months. Provides early signals of potential instability.
  • Refrigeration — samples stored at 4°C ± 2°C. Checks whether cold storage causes crystallisation, phase separation or other changes.
  • Freeze-thaw cycling — samples cycled between -10°C and 25°C for multiple cycles. Simulates shipping and storage temperature fluctuations.
  • Light exposure — samples exposed to controlled light to evaluate photostability, especially important for products with natural extracts or colorants.

At each evaluation point, parameters such as appearance, colour, odour, pH, viscosity, foam quality, foam stability and physical stability are assessed. Samples should be stored in the real final packaging, because packaging interactions can affect stability.

Preservation and Microbiological Considerations

Water-containing shampoos require appropriate microbial control and preservation. Preservation strategy should be considered alongside formulation composition, pH, surfactant type, packaging, manufacturing and intended use.

Shampoos can be particularly challenging to preserve because surfactants can interact with preservatives, reducing their effectiveness. Some surfactant-based shampoos are difficult to preserve and may require careful selection of preservative systems.

Preservative efficacy testing — also called challenge testing, typically performed per ISO 11930 — evaluates whether a preservative system can prevent microbial growth over time. This involves inoculating the product with known microorganisms and monitoring their survival.

An important consideration for shampoos: Pseudomonas gergoviae, a microorganism that can survive in surfactant-rich environments, must be explicitly targeted in challenge testing because it is not always included in the standard ISO 11930 panel. Standard batch release microbiological testing may not detect it, so challenge testing is the only way to confirm the preservative system is effective against it.

Packaging Compatibility for Shampoos

Packaging is often considered too late in the development process. For certain shampoo concepts, packaging can have an important relationship with product stability and consumer experience. Considerations may include air exposure, container compatibility, pump or flip-top performance, product dispensing, material compatibility and consumer handling.

A technically sound formulation still needs packaging that is appropriate for the product. For example, a shampoo with a high concentration of natural extracts may need opaque packaging to protect against light degradation. A thick, creamy shampoo may need a pump that dispenses evenly. A thin, watery shampoo may need a flip-top bottle that controls flow.

Beyond the Lab

Shampoo Product Development From Concept to Manufacturing

Shampoo product development extends beyond laboratory formulation. A commercially prepared product may require support across several stages.

Quick answer: Shampoo product development follows a pathway from concept and surfactant strategy through formulation, prototype development, evaluation, stability and testing, packaging considerations and manufacturing scale-up. At the development stage, the formulation also needs to be considered from a manufacturing perspective.

A formula that works in a small laboratory batch may require further optimisation before larger-scale production. Mixing conditions, processing order, raw material handling, equipment and manufacturing parameters can influence the final product. This is where formulation and product development need to work together.

Manufacturing Scale-Up for Shampoos

Scale-up is the step of moving a formula from a small lab batch to commercial production. Batch size, mixing equipment, heating and cooling rates and addition order can all change the finished product.

Shampoo scale-up has particular challenges. Viscosity is highly sensitive to scale-up because mixing speed, shear and temperature affect how thickeners and surfactants interact. A shampoo that has the perfect viscosity in a 1 kg lab batch may be too thin or too thick in a 100 kg production batch. This is why viscosity is often adjusted at the end of the manufacturing process, after the batch has cooled.

The addition order of ingredients also matters. Surfactants, conditioning agents and thickeners need to be added in the right sequence and at the right temperature. If a cationic polymer is added before the surfactant is fully dissolved, it may not disperse properly and could form lumps.

Pilot batches test the scaled-up process before full commercial production. These batches identify potential issues and allow adjustments before committing to large-scale manufacturing. Documentation of process parameters — temperature, mixing speed, addition rate, holding time — ensures the product can be reproduced consistently.

Technology Transfer

Technology transfer turns lab methods into factory instructions. The formulator prepares a detailed manufacturing procedure covering ingredient weights, phase order, mixing conditions, pH adjustment, preservation addition and packaging instructions.

Clear documentation is essential. The manufacturer needs to understand not just what to do, but why each step matters. For example, if a surfactant is sensitive to high shear, the manufacturer needs to know the maximum mixing speed. If a preservative needs to be added at a specific temperature, that needs to be documented.

Working With Contract Manufacturers

Choosing the right manufacturer for your shampoo depends on product category, production scale, equipment, capabilities, minimum order quantities and technical requirements. A formulation consultant can help you evaluate manufacturing options from a product and technical perspective.

Consider the manufacturer's experience with hair care products. Shampoo manufacturing requires equipment for mixing surfactants, adjusting viscosity and filling bottles or tubes. Not all cosmetic manufacturers have this capability.

Minimum order quantities are also a consideration. A high MOQ may not suit a new brand with limited initial demand. A very low MOQ may not have the capacity to scale up later. Communication is key — clear specifications, detailed batch records and regular updates ensure the product meets expectations.

Indian Manufacturing Considerations

India has a growing contract manufacturing sector for hair care products. Many manufacturers specialise in shampoos, conditioners and hair oils. But capabilities vary widely. Some manufacturers are equipped for large-volume production but have limited formulation expertise. Others offer both formulation and manufacturing services.

When evaluating manufacturers in India, consider their experience with hair care, their quality systems, their certifications, their minimum order quantities and their ability to scale. Shampoo manufacturing requires close attention to viscosity control and batch uniformity, so the manufacturer's equipment and processes matter.

Regulatory Considerations for Shampoos

Regulatory requirements vary by market and should be considered during development, not only after formulation is complete.

Quick answer: Shampoo regulations depend on the country, product category, ingredients, claims and how the product is sold. In India, shampoos are regulated under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and the Cosmetics Rules, 2020, and must comply with BIS specification IS 4707. Plan for them during formulation, because a restricted ingredient or an over-stated claim found late can force expensive changes.

India: CDSCO and BIS Standards

In India, shampoos are regulated under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and the Cosmetics Rules, 2020. The Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO) and state FDAs oversee compliance.

Shampoos must comply with Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) specification IS 4707:2016, which covers parameters such as pH, detergent concentration, heavy metal limits (lead ≤20 ppm, arsenic ≤5 ppm) and microbial limits (total bacterial count ≤100 CFU/g).

The labelling requirements under the Cosmetic Rules 2020 are particularly relevant for shampoos: ingredient listing must follow INCI nomenclature, and claims such as "sulfate-free," "paraben-free," "anti-dandruff" or "for curly hair" are subject to substantiation requirements that have been increasingly enforced by the CDSCO and the Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI). The ASCI has issued guidelines requiring that benefit claims for hair cosmetics be supported by either in-vitro sensory panel data or published clinical references.

Imported shampoos require registration with CDSCO before they can be sold. The registration process requires product documentation, safety data and compliance with Indian standards.

A critical point for shampoo labelling: BIS has different standards for soap-based and synthetic detergent-based shampoos. The label must clearly indicate whether the product is soap-based or synthetic detergent-based, so the analyst can carry out the correct tests.

International Markets

In the European Union, shampoos are regulated as cosmetics under Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009. This requires a Cosmetic Product Safety Report (CPSR), a Product Information File (PIF), and notification to the Cosmetic Products Notification Portal (CPNP). Ingredient restrictions, labelling requirements and claims rules differ from India.

In the United States, shampoos are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. Shampoos that make drug-like claims (such as treating dandruff) may be regulated as drugs.

In the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, shampoos are regulated by the Gulf Standardisation Organisation (GSO). Products must comply with GSO standards for ingredients, labelling and safety. In Southeast Asia, each country has its own regulatory framework, though ASEAN has harmonised cosmetic regulations to some extent.

If you are developing a shampoo for multiple markets, it is more efficient to identify your target markets early and formulate with the most restrictive requirements in mind. Changing a formula after it has been finalised is more expensive than designing for compliance from the start.

Claims and Positioning

A cosmetic shampoo generally cleanses, perfumes, protects, changes appearance or keeps the body in good condition. Wording that suggests treating or preventing disease usually pushes a product out of the cosmetic category and into drug regulation.

Common cosmetic claims for shampoos include "gentle," "hydrating," "volumising," "color-protecting," "sulfate-free," "paraben-free," "dermatologically tested" and "for curly hair." Each of these claims has specific implications and may require supporting evidence.

Claims should be developed in parallel with the formulation. If the formula cannot support the claim, either the formula or the claim needs to change. Addressing this early prevents disappointment and rework.

This section is general information, not legal advice. Always confirm current requirements with the relevant authority or a qualified regulatory professional.

Frequently Asked Questions About Shampoo Formulation

Shampoo formulation is the process of designing a hair cleansing product that removes dirt, oil, product buildup and environmental pollutants from the hair and scalp while maintaining hair manageability, softness and scalp comfort. It involves decisions about surfactant systems, conditioning agents, pH, viscosity, preservation, fragrance and packaging compatibility.

Common primary surfactants include sodium laureth sulfate (SLES), sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), sodium lauroyl methyl isethionate, sodium cocoyl isethionate and alpha-olefin sulfonate. Secondary surfactants include cocamidopropyl betaine, coco-glucoside and lauryl glucoside. The choice depends on the desired cleansing strength, foam profile, mildness and hair type.

Sulfate shampoos use sulfated surfactants such as sodium laureth sulfate or sodium lauryl sulfate, which provide strong cleansing and rich foam but can be stripping for some hair types. Sulfate-free shampoos use alternative surfactants such as glucosides, isethionates or sarcosinates. These are often milder but may foam less and feel different on the hair.

Shampoo pH typically ranges from 4.5 to 6.0, which is close to the natural pH of the scalp and hair. This slightly acidic range helps maintain the scalp's moisture barrier, keeps the hair cuticle smooth and improves shine. Some clarifying shampoos may have a higher pH, while color-protecting shampoos often aim for a lower pH.

Common conditioning agents include cationic polymers (polyquaternium-7, polyquaternium-10, guar hydroxypropyltrimonium chloride), silicones (dimethicone, amodimethicone), protein hydrolysates, panthenol and fatty alcohols. Cationic polymers deposit on the hair surface, reducing static and improving combability. Silicones provide slip, shine and smoothness.

Water-containing shampoos require preservation to control microbial growth. Common preservative systems include phenoxyethanol, sodium benzoate, potassium sorbate, methylisothiazolinone and blends. Preservation can be challenging because surfactants can interact with preservatives. Preservative efficacy testing (challenge testing per ISO 11930) confirms whether the chosen system works.

Stability testing involves storing samples under controlled conditions (room temperature, elevated temperature, refrigeration, freeze-thaw cycles) and evaluating appearance, colour, odour, pH, viscosity, foam quality and physical stability over time. Accelerated testing at 40°C provides early signals, but real-time data remains the reference for shelf life.

Conditioners are conditioning products based on cationic surfactants, fatty alcohols and silicones that deposit on the hair surface to improve softness, detangling and shine. Two-in-one shampoos combine both functions in one product.

Natural preservatives may be less effective and require combination with other preservation strategies. Establish the intended certification standard early.

A straightforward cleansing shampoo may move faster than a sophisticated anti-dandruff or color-protecting formula. Plan in months rather than weeks, and allow time for stability observation and refinement.

Scale-up is transferring a laboratory shampoo formula to commercial production. Batch size, mixing equipment, heating and cooling rates and addition order can all change the finished product. Shampoo viscosity is particularly sensitive to scale-up. Pilot batches help identify issues before full production.

Packaging depends on the product format. Bottles with flip-top caps or pumps suit liquid shampoos. Tubes suit thicker formulas or travel sizes. Airless pumps protect sensitive formulas. The packaging must protect the formula, dispense the right amount, and suit consumer usage habits in the shower.
